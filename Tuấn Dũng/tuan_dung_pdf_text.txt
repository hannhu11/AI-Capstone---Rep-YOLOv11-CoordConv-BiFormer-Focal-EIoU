SHWD-YOLO11s: Training-Policy Optimization
and Cross-Domain Evaluation for Safety Helmet
Detection
Tuan Dung
Affiliation and e-mail to be supplied before submission
Abstract—Automated safety-helmet monitoring requires both
accurate localization and real-time inference under substantial
domain shift. This work studies two three-head YOLO11s vari-
ants trained on the Safety Helmet Wearing Dataset (SHWD):
SHWD-YOLO11s-DA, based on a data-augmentation fine-tuning
policy, and SHWD-YOLO11s-LO, which modifies the box-loss
weight, MixUp rate, and cosine learning-rate schedule. At 960-
pixel validation resolution, the DA variant reaches 96.111%
mAP50 and 63.513% mAP50–95, whereas LO reaches 96.148%
and 63.760%, respectively. Measured batch-one PyTorch FP32
throughput on a Kaggle Tesla T4 is 48.7 FPS for DA and 47.0
FPS for LO. We further evaluate both checkpoints on five helmet-
detection datasets using explicitly reported official, standardized,
or deterministic-holdout protocols. LO improves mAP50–95 on
four of five datasets, with its largest external-domain gain of
1.63 percentage points on Hard Hat Workers, but performance
remains limited on SHEL5K. These results show that training-
policy optimization provides modest in-domain gains and non-
uniform cross-domain benefits; they do not establish a universal
improvement across domains.
Index Terms—safety helmet detection, YOLO11s, data aug-
mentation, loss optimization, cross-domain generalization, real-
time inference
I. INTRODUCTION
Construction-site monitoring must identify both workers and
protective helmets despite occlusion, crowding, illumination
changes, and small object scale. A detector intended for
operational use must therefore balance localization accuracy,
recall, and inference throughput. YOLO11 provides a compact
one-stage detection baseline suitable for this setting [1].
This study does not introduce an unverified re-parameterized
architecture. Instead, it examines two evidence-backed train-
ing configurations applied to a checkpoint-native, three-head
YOLO11s detector. The first configuration, SHWD-YOLO11s-
DA, uses a hard-case augmentation fine-tuning policy. The sec-
ond, SHWD-YOLO11s-LO, changes several training-policy
factors: the box-loss weight increases from 7.5 to 10.0, MixUp
changes from 0 to 0.15, and cosine learning-rate scheduling is
enabled. Because these factors change jointly and execution of
a custom Focal-EIoU implementation has not been indepen-
dently verified on every distributed worker, we attribute the
second configuration to policy-level loss optimization rather
than to Focal-EIoU alone.
The contributions are: (1) an artifact-grounded comparison
of DA and LO at 960-pixel resolution; (2) measured deploy-
ment results across GPU FP32, GPU FP16, CPU ONNX, and
a CCTV-oriented code path; and (3) a protocol-aware cross-
domain benchmark covering SHWD, GDUT-HWD, SHEL5K,
Hard Hat Workers, and SFCHD.
II. METHODOLOGY
A. Model Variants
Both variants retain the native YOLO11s detection topology
and predict two classes, hat and person. SHWD-YOLO11s-DA
uses box weight 7.5, no MixUp, and a non-cosine learning-
rate schedule. SHWD-YOLO11s-LO uses box weight 10.0,
MixUp probability 0.15, and cosine scheduling. Both recorded
training configurations use an image size of 960 × 960 and
device identifiers 0 and 1.
B. Evaluation Metrics
Precision P is the fraction of predicted positives that are
correct, while recall R is the fraction of ground-truth objects
recovered. AP is integrated over the precision–recall curve. We
report mAP50 at IoU 0.5 and the stricter mAP50–95 averaged
over IoU thresholds 0.50:0.05:0.95. The latter is the primary
localization metric.
C. Protocol Separation
Internal validation metrics are read from the best row of
each run’s results.csv, selected by mAP50–95. Cross-
domain results use the protocol assigned to each dataset in
the benchmark: official, standardized, or deterministic holdout.
These protocol classes are not pooled into a single overall
score because their splits and preprocessing are not necessarily
exchangeable.

GDUT-HWD Standardized and SHEL5K Standardized use

Workers [2] uses a deterministic holdout rather than an offi-
cial split. SFCHD uses the official protocol recorded by the

On VOC2028, LO preserves mAP50 (+0.0001) and raises
mAP50–95 by 0.0015; recall increases by 0.0059 while preci-
sion falls by 0.0052. On GDUT-HWD, LO improves mAP50
and mAP50–95 by 0.0067 and 0.0110, respectively, despite a
0.0098 recall reduction. SHEL5K is the weakest domain: both
variants have recall near 0.375 and mAP50–95 below 0.25. LO
increases SHEL5K mAP50 by 0.0024 but reduces mAP50–95
by 0.0062.
The largest external localization gain occurs on Hard Hat
Workers, where LO raises mAP50–95 by 0.0163 and mAP50
by 0.0067, with a 0.0029 recall increase and a 0.0070 pre-
cision reduction. On SFCHD, LO improves recall by 0.0060,
mAP50 by 0.0074, and mAP50–95 by 0.0103, while precision
decreases by 0.0165. Overall, LO improves mAP50–95 on four
of five datasets but does not dominate every metric or every
domain. The large source-to-external decline, particularly on
SHEL5K, demonstrates unresolved domain shift.
IV. THREATS TO VALIDITY
The DA and LO configurations alter multiple training
factors simultaneously; their difference is not a single-factor
loss ablation. No causal Focal-EIoU claim is therefore made.
Cross-domain rows employ heterogeneous protocols and must
not be averaged without sample-aware normalization. The de-
ployment report provides measured values, but core-inference
and CCTV paths are separate benchmark implementations.
Finally, model parameters and GFLOPs are omitted until they
are measured from the exact archived checkpoints.
V. CONCLUSION
SHWD-YOLO11s-DA and SHWD-YOLO11s-LO both pro-
vide real-time GPU FP32 inference at 960-pixel resolution. LO
achieves the higher internal mAP50–95, with a modest 0.247-
point gain over DA, and improves cross-domain mAP50–95
on four of five evaluated datasets. These improvements are
accompanied by small precision–recall trade-offs and do not
eliminate the pronounced domain gap on SHEL5K. Future
work should verify custom-loss execution on every distributed
worker, perform paired multi-seed loss ablations, and analyze
localization errors by object scale and occlusion.
